## The spine (put this in references/philosophy.md)

The whole method works **inside-out**: why → audience → goal → content → visuals last. Slides are left until (almost) last on purpose — you should be able to convince on words alone.

Core lines, in his words:
- "A pitch is a story with a goal." Stories communicate; a pitch communicates *in order to move someone*.
- "Your job is not to convince everyone. Your job is to find the people who believe as you believe, and make it easy for them to say yes."
- "Most pitches fail because they speak to the neocortex. The croc brain is the gatekeeper." (Pitch Anything / Sinek)
- "Show me something you don't like about a deck and I'll show you the fear that caused it." Every bad artefact is fear made visible.
- "Your deck is for your audience, not for you." Not a crutch, not a jetpack.
- "Every slide is the answer to a question."
- "Pitch early, pitch often." Lean startup applied to storytelling: build → get feedback → iterate.
- "Feedback is a gift, not a request for change." (Corey Ford)
- "The goal isn't perfection, it's preparedness."
- "Fear is energy. Energy can't be destroyed, only transformed."
- "Your goal is not to speak without anxiety. It is to positively influence your audience."
- "Good enough is good enough." Minimum effective dose, not mastery.

Two frameworks he built, both to be honoured:
- **5 Ps** — Prepare, Plot, Practise, Picture, Polish (then Pitch: Before / During / After)
- **S.T.O.R.Y.** — Start with why, Tell the story, Own the visuals, Rehearse, You are your pitch

The 3 Ds map cleanly: Define = Prepare + Plot + Start with why + Tell the story. Design = Picture + Own the visuals. Deliver = Practise + Polish + Rehearse + You are your pitch + Before/During/After.

## DEFINE

### finding-your-why
- Source: Ch 02 [Storytelling], Ch x, V2 Ch 4 Start with Why, composite Prologue.
- The three questions: What do you do? What are you passionate about? What is it about this that makes your heart sing? (The math-app CEO example — the third answer is the one.)
- Fill-in: "I ___ so that ___."
- Why for three things: yourself (passion), your audience (why should they care), your pitch (goal).
- Evidence he cites: Sinek golden circle; Talk Like TED's 500-investor survey (passion trumps all); oxytocin/DARPA on stories; law of diffusion — your audience are early adopters who operate from why.
- Rule: "Money, happiness, freedom are results, not reasons."

### knowing-the-room-before-you-write
- Source: Ch 05 [Prepare] 0.2, Structure: Prepare.
- The 5 Ws: Why (goal), Who (audience), What (type of pitch), Where (location), When (date/time/duration).
- Goal: pick ONE using the focusing question — "What's the one thing I can achieve with this pitch such that achieving it makes everything else easier or unnecessary?" Then the mission statement: "I want to <goal> in order to <outcome>. My pitch will help my audience <goal> so that they <outcome>." Double-check: "Do I actually need to pitch to get this?"
- Audience: "Everyone is never an answer." Five questions (demographics, why are they here, what keeps them up at night, how can you solve *their* problem, how to reach them). Google-stalk them, never tell them. Smaller audience = deeper research; larger = persona.
- Where: elevator / coffee shop / boardroom / stage / Skype — each changes the pitch. Visit the space if you can.
- When: "It always takes 3x as long to prepare as you think." The 4:20am India Skype story.
- Output: the **preparation statement** — "I will be pitching to <person> with the intention of <goal>, on <date/time/tz>, in <location>. I will have <n> minutes. My goal is X so that Y."

### narrowing-too-many-ideas-into-one
- Source: Structure: Plot, Ch 05, The One Thing.
- The focusing question again, applied per section: "What's the ONE thing my audience needs to know about <section> such that by knowing it everything else is easier or unnecessary?"
- Test: "If the audience only knew these 12 points, would I achieve my goal?"

### turning-beats-into-a-narrative
- Source: Structure: Plot, Ch 06 [Plot], Outline V2 Ch 5.
- Plot = "a list of the key points an audience needs to hear in order to achieve your goal." Start with the end in mind. Use the 10/20/30 twelve headings as scaffolding.
- Under each heading ask: "In order for my audience to <goal>, what do they need to know / feel / think about <section>?"
- The **"So what?" test**: after each point, ask so what; if the next sentence doesn't answer it, there's a gap. (The Google-team example.)
- Openings: start with the problem; get them to *feel* it.
- Four questions on reread: does it make the point, is it clear, is it as simple as possible, is it as short as possible, did I leave out anything necessary.
- Watch for word repetition, clichés, useless adverbs.

### talking-your-way-to-a-first-draft
- Source: Ch 06 [Plot] — the "no words" technique.
- Premise: "The problem with writing is that you suck at it. But you're amazing at talking." Speaking is not writing; stop writing something meant to be spoken.
- Method: hand a friend the outline, why statement, persona and goal → they interview you for 1–2 hours → record → transcribe → that's draft one.
- Interviewer rules: make them comfortable, be interested, start simple, let them speak, push for specificity ("tell me about a time when…"), sit in the audience's head, find the "littlebigs" (specific before sweeping).
- Interviewee rules: relax, go with the flow, tell stories, give freely (nothing is wasted), speak simply, have fun.
- Interviewer must NOT be a cofounder — they know too much. Pick someone like the eventual audience.
- Precedent: Churchill dictated.
- "All first drafts are shit." (Hemingway) "The first draft is just you telling yourself the story." (Pratchett)

## DESIGN

### storyboarding-before-you-open-keynote
- Source: Ch 08 [Picture 0.3] Step 1; Old versions 08.
- "Analogue or bust." Pen & paper / whiteboard first — removes the tool as a hurdle.
- Draw 10–12 boxes. Each: (a) core story, (b) slide message, (c) slide content.
- Write the slide message **from the audience's point of view**: "These guys have built tons in 3 months with no cash — what could they do with my money?"
- Deliver the spoken pitch while pointing at the boxes. Transitions matter as much as slides.
- Brainstorm rules: no judgement, timebox, wild ideas, every idea has worth, yes-and. Red-dot vote.
- Three kinds of information to pull from the narrative: complicated stuff a visual makes clearer; imagery that makes them feel; social proof you can show. "Anything you can show instead of tell."

### turning-story-beats-into-slides
- Source: Ch 08 Design section — the design rules.
- Three-step: Outline → Design (simple shapes and text, no stock templates) → Refine.
- Rules: simplify layout (≤8 templates); first things first (hierarchy); 3-second rule (glance media); images for impact, never stock; ≤3 lines of text, font size = oldest audience member's age ÷ 2 (Kawasaki); ≤2 fonts; limit bullets; simple backgrounds; logo out of the way; 3-tone colour scheme (brand + black + white); show insights not data (tell the truth, don't fudge axes, get to the point); no animations unless explaining a concept.
- Two kinds of deck: presentation deck vs leave-behind deck — different rules.
- "Don't commit suislide." (Duarte)

### deciding-whether-to-demo
- Source: Old versions 08 [Picture] — the Corey Ford / Matter story.
- Position: if it can be demo'd, demo it. "Showing them something real grounds the pitch in reality."
- How: show a narrative use case, not the literal product in the room. Be obvious about the switch: "Let's see it in action."
- Fallbacks by scenario: no product yet → hi-fi prototype walkthrough; product can't be demo'd live → screen recording; service → testimonials and stories; idea only → paint before/after vividly, stack credibility indicators; no wifi → embedded screencast sized to the slide.

### cutting-what-doesnt-earn-its-place
- Source: Ch 08 Refine; Structure: Polish (K.I.S.S.); Ch 06 reread questions.
- "Kill your darlings." For everything on every slide: why is this needed? does it help communicate the main point? does it help my big goal? does it have more than one point (squint test)? If no, remove.
- Fluff = ers/ums, jargon, unnecessarily long words. "If you had to Google it, you have no business using it."
- Then stop: "Until things are good enough. Then step back and leave it alone."

## DELIVER

### rehearsing-until-they-hear-what-you-said
- Source: Ch 07 [Practise] 0.2, Structure: Practise, Fearless L09.
- Deliberate practice, not repetition: pick a goal → practise out loud → get feedback → repeat with a different audience.
- Inside-out audiences: **self** (table read seated, then recorded on your feet) → **friends** (one question: "What are the three things you remember?") → **group** (the **Wish / Question / Love / Hate** sheet) → **strangers** ($5 coffee card; the script; "the mom test").
- Record everything (with permission). You will hate your voice — here's the physics of why.
- How much: MED. 20 hours of deliberate practice ≈ 60 reps of a 10–20 min pitch. Patrick Stewart reads a script 100 times. "Don't let the investors be the first strangers you pitch to."
- Result: a pitch that sounds natural, resonates, is battle-tested.

### taking-feedback-without-taking-it-personally
- Source: Ch 07 "How to deal with feedback."
- Three steps: say thank you (all feedback is a gift); separate the information from the emotion ("hated her voice" = spoke too fast); decide what's useful and integrate it (the investor-feedback-that-was-wrong story).
- "Feedback is a gift, not a request for change." You don't have to act on all of it. Trust your gut.
- Teach the audience how to give it: "When you said X, it wasn't clear to me…" Set expectations (interrupt or hold?). Make them fill every quadrant.

### preparing-for-what-could-go-wrong
- Source: Ch 09 [Polish] What-ifs; Ch 10 dry run; Ch 11 [During]; Outline V2 Ch 8.
- The **What-Ifs** exercise: four lists — questions that could be asked, tech that could fail, things you could do wrong (forget lines), things that could change (lineup, venue). Pitch to people and have them list every hole. Plug the biggest.
- "The types of fails are not infinite." Fear comes from the unknown; every scenario you plan for is one less unknown.
- The **dry run**: recreate the room (dining table, empty chairs), act as if it's real, note every friction point. For big stages, treat the tech run as the real thing and ask for what you need (mic height, water).
- Control what you can (your laptop's projector settings) so you have headspace for what you can't.
- Tech fails: three kinds of pitches — failed, failed and lied, yet to fail. Breathe, give the crew a second, then put the clicker down and keep going. Your words should carry it.
- Interruptions are interest. Answer, then resume.

### getting-through-the-day-of
- Source: Ch 10 [before].
- Night before: do less. Pack everything (his checklist: deck in multiple formats, hard copies, chargers, adapters, demo backup, leave-behinds, clothes laid out, address printed, transit checked). Block the front door with the bag. Don't change anything. Visualisation routine. Sleep.
- Mindset: "They want you to succeed." (The Kevin Spacey casting-director story.) Nobody shows up hoping to be bored.
- Morning: dressed, breakfast, early ("on time is 15 minutes late"). Voice and body warm-up (RSC routine). Do eat light 2h before; don't caffeinate; avoid dairy; check tech; don't change anything; think about your why.
- Dress: "You are not Zuck." Dress slightly better than your audience; practise in the outfit. Thin-slicing (Gladwell).

### handling-the-questions-after
- Source: Ch 12 [After], Outline V2 Ch 11/13/14.
- Never say "great question." Let them finish, pause 2–3 seconds (reads as confidence), answer the question they asked — ask for clarification if needed. Clear, direct, simple. Eye contact.
- Don't know? Say so, promise to follow up. "Making a mistake is ok, bullshitting is not." The VC-gangbang story.
- Stupid or repeated questions: never make anyone feel bad. "As I mentioned earlier…" then offer to take it offline.
- Difficult questioners: rise above, keep calm — they may be testing you. Passion good, shouting not; disagreeing expected, insulting not.
- Follow-up: handwritten thank-you immediately; email the leave-behind deck.
- Placeholder: frame control / the 6 types of VC (never written).

### calming-the-fear-before-you-speak
- Source: udemy Fearless Speaking 005 (most complete), Change your mind, Ch 07 "secret to curing stage fright."
- Fear is learned, therefore controllable. It's adrenaline — energy — and every symptom has a flip side (racing mind = ideas; racing heart = focus; tense muscles = ready).
- The **Big 6** fears: failure, being judged, appearing nervous, going blank, lacking talent, how it will feel. Name yours.
- Limiting beliefs to challenge: fear is the enemy → it's a signal you're doing something that matters; speaking is a performance → it's a conversation (use questions); it's about me → it's about them (you can't focus inward and outward at once).
- Body: posture drill (arms up, exhale, ribcage stays), diaphragmatic breathing (hand on belly), speak on the breath; smile / smize; act-as-if and power poses (Cuddy, Wiseman); get present (tongue twisters, count backwards, music).
- Practice cures it: systematic desensitisation. Daily micro-drills — "you go first," compliments, disagreements.
- Ritual before going on: breathe, power pose, smile, warm the voice.
- Speakeasy line worth keeping: "It's easier to share an emoji than an emotion."
