# Storytelling — the philosophy

This is the single source of truth. Every skill in the pack points here before it does anything. It is domain-agnostic: no slide advice, no audience tactics, no design language. Those live in the lenses and the tactical skills.

Format for every section, as in everything in this pack: the belief in one line → why it holds (one reason, one story, or one citation) → what it demands of you.

---

## What storytelling is for

Storytelling is how you move someone from what they believe now to what you need them to believe. It is not information transfer. A story communicates a sequence and a point of view; a pitch does the same thing with a goal — and every review, readout, crit, talk, memo and interview is a pitch.

Written by someone who spent years failing at this first — a trained actor who assumed he didn't need to work at it, lost a competition to a team with no product, and then spent a decade fixing it for himself and for hundreds of founders and designers.

## The twelve beliefs

### 1. Belief is the currency
**Belief:** Your work lives or dies on whether the room believes in it. Belief is not a by-product of craft.
**Why:** Investors invest in stories, not businesses. Executives decide with the old brain and rationalise with the new one. A better artefact does not beat a better story; it never has.
**Demands:** Before you open the file, know what you need the room to believe when you stop talking.

### 2. Two steps to belief
**Belief:** Know what you believe. Then express it through a story. Skip the first and nothing else works.
**Why:** The math-app founder's third answer — "I'm helping kids see the world in a new way" — is the one investors funded. The first two were features.
**Demands:** Complete "I ___ so that ___" in one breath, with no feature in the second half, before drafting anything.

### 3. Inside-out
**Belief:** Why → audience → goal → content → visuals → delivery. Always in that order.
**Why:** Great communicators start with why (Sinek). Most presenters start with what they made and work backwards, which is why most presentations are inventories.
**Demands:** Any skill that touches slides checks that the narrative exists first. Any skill that touches narrative checks that the why exists first.

### 4. Slides last, on purpose
**Belief:** Everything is built before the deck exists so the deck can never become a crutch.
**Why:** If you can't convince on words alone, the deck is doing your job for you — and the moment the projector dies, so are you. The first three Ps (prepare, plot, practise) produce a spoken story that works without a single visual.
**Demands:** Be able to deliver the whole thing with the screen off. If you can't, you're not ready to design slides.

### 5. Two realities
**Belief:** Every story has a world before and a world after. The story carries the room from one to the other.
**Why:** Duarte's "what is / what could be"; McKee's gap; the contrast that gives a narrative its engine. Without a before, the after has no weight. Without an after, there's no reason to listen.
**Demands:** Name the before and the after in one sentence each. If either is missing, you don't have a story yet. (This is also the visual brief: FROM on the left, TO on the right.)

### 6. You're hardwired to be bad at this
**Belief:** Two evolutionary reasons, plus one occupational one.
**Why:** You know too much about your own work to see what a stranger needs (curse of knowledge). You speak to the neocortex when the croc brain is the gatekeeper — present numbers and they question you; present belief and they ask for the numbers. And if you make things for a living, you were trained to let the work speak for itself. It doesn't.
**Demands:** Assume your first draft over-explains the how and under-explains the why. Get it in front of someone who doesn't know the work before you trust it.

### 7. A throughline outranks a collection of good ideas
**Belief:** One thread, or nothing lands.
**Why:** Cognitive load is real (Sweller). Olson's "and, and, and" is how competent people lose rooms — everything true, nothing memorable. The focusing question from *The One Thing* exists because five good points are worse than one.
**Demands:** State the story as a single sentence — and, but, therefore — before you write beats. Everything that doesn't serve it gets cut or demoted.

### 8. The audience, not you
**Belief:** The deck is for them. The goal is what they need to hear, not what you want to say. And this is also the fear cure.
**Why:** We can't focus inwards and outwards at the same time. Every bad slide is a fear made visible: too much text is fear of forgetting; beautiful-but-empty is fear you won't be believed; reading the slides is fear of the audience. Show me something wrong with a deck and I'll show you the fear that caused it.
**Demands:** Write every slide's message in the audience's voice, as the thought you want in their head. When you feel afraid, put your attention on them.

### 9. Fear is energy
**Belief:** Fear is learned, therefore controllable. It never goes away, and shouldn't.
**Why:** It's adrenaline. A racing mind is also a mind full of ideas; a racing heart is also focus; tense muscles are also readiness. Energy can't be destroyed, only transformed. The goal is not to speak without anxiety; it is to positively influence your audience.
**Demands:** Greet it — "this is me nervous because I'm about to do something that matters" — then breathe, and put the energy into the room.

### 10. Practise is the key to all of it
**Belief:** Deliberate, out loud, in front of others, iterated on feedback. Not line-learning.
**Why:** It hones the story, cures stage fright by systematic desensitisation, and makes you authentic because you know it cold. Great speakers are not born. Jobs practised. Everyone practised.
**Demands:** Pick a goal for the session, say it out loud, get feedback, repeat with a different audience. Don't let the real room be the first strangers you pitch to.

### 11. Lean applied to storytelling
**Belief:** Pitch early, pitch often. Pitch → feedback → iterate → repeat.
**Why:** Build-measure-learn works on narratives exactly as it works on products. There is a minimum effective dose — roughly twenty hours of deliberate practise — and there is a point of diminishing returns where more practise makes you robotic.
**Demands:** Get out of the building. Then let good enough be good enough, step back, and leave it alone.

### 12. You are already an actor
**Belief:** You play lover, child, leader and colleague fluently every day. The presenter simply defaults to The Timid Speaker instead of choosing a role.
**Why:** Mirror neurons: if you look confident they feel confident. Presence is a choice of role, and choosing it is a craft — the same craft you already use in every other room of your life.
**Demands:** Decide who you are in this room before you walk in. Not a performance with a right way to do it — a conversation, from the person who owns the work.

## One adopted rule that belongs with the beliefs

**Feedback is a gift, not a request for change.** (Corey Ford.) Say thank you. Separate the information from the emotion — "I hated her voice" meant "she spoke too fast." Decide what serves the goal and integrate that; trust your gut on the rest.

## The five questions

Asked in order, before any drafting. Every tactical skill starts here.

1. Who is this for, and what do they already believe?
2. What is the one thing they must believe by the end?
3. What is the before, and what is the after?
4. What proof makes the change credible?
5. What shape does this audience need it delivered in?

## The diagnostics

Quick tests any skill can run against a draft.

- **Why-check** — can you say "I did X so that Y" without a feature in the Y?
- **Two-realities test** — is there a clear before, a clear after, and a turn between them?
- **Everyone test** — if the audience is "everyone," the audience isn't defined.
- **Throughline test** — one sentence, and-but-therefore; does every beat serve it?
- **So-what test** — after every point, ask so what; if the next line doesn't answer, there's a gap.
- **Passion-vs-metrics test** — if it opens with numbers, it opens on the wrong brain.
- **Three-things test** — does what listeners recall match what you intended?
- **Cue-card test** — if you can't deliver without the deck, practise more.
- **Bluff test** — any answer you couldn't back with a source afterwards is a bluff.
- **Good-enough test** — roughly twenty hours or sixty reps, or a version that does the job even if you don't love it.

## Sources this philosophy leans on

Sinek (Start with Why); Duarte (Resonate); McKee (Story); Olson (ABT, "and, and, and"); Keller (The One Thing); Ries (Lean Startup); Kaufman (The First 20 Hours); Ferriss (minimum effective dose); Ericsson (deliberate practice); Klaff (Pitch Anything — croc brain); Green & Brock (narrative transportation); Sweller (cognitive load); Corey Ford. Where the corpus cites a figure that hasn't been verified, the skills keep the point and drop the number.
