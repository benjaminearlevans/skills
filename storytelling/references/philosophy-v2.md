# Storytelling — the philosophy

This is the single source of truth. Every skill in the pack points here before it does anything. It is written for designers who present their work — IC through Head of Design — in the rooms where design is judged: the crit, the design review, the exec readout, the roadmap conversation, the all-hands or conference talk, the portfolio interview. No slide rules and no drills live here; those belong to the tactical skills.

Format for every section, as everywhere in this pack: the belief in one line → why it holds (one reason, one story, or one citation) → what it demands of you.

Where a story is marked **[your story]**, the corpus names it but hasn't written it. Those are yours to supply.

---

## What storytelling is for

Storytelling is how you move a room from what it believes now to what you need it to believe. It is not information transfer, and it is not a tour of the work. A story communicates a sequence and a point of view; a pitch does the same thing to get a decision — and every crit, review, readout, talk, memo and interview is a pitch.

Written by a trained actor who assumed he didn't need to work at this, lost a room to someone with a clearer story and worse work, and then spent a decade fixing it — for himself, and then for the designers he led at Airbnb, PayPal and Cash App.

## The twelve beliefs

### 1. Belief is the currency
**Belief:** Your work lives or dies on whether the room believes in it. Belief is not a by-product of craft.
**Why:** Executives decide with the old brain and rationalise with the new one. A better artefact does not beat a better story; it never has. Every designer has watched a weaker design ship because someone else told a better story about it. **[your story: the review lost to a clearer story.]**
**Demands:** Before you open the file, know what you need the room to believe when you stop talking — and say it in one sentence.

### 2. Two steps to belief
**Belief:** Know what you believe. Then express it through a story. Skip the first and nothing else works.
**Why:** Ask a designer why they made something and the first answer is a feature ("we moved the CTA above the fold"). The second is a metric ("to lift conversion"). The third — "so a first-time user never has to wonder what to do next" — is the one the room can believe in, because it's the only one with a person in it.
**Demands:** Complete "I designed ___ so that ___" in one breath, with no feature and no metric in the second half, before drafting anything.

### 3. Inside-out
**Belief:** Why → audience → goal → content → visuals → delivery. Always in that order.
**Why:** Great communicators start with why (Sinek). Most design presentations start with the screens and work backwards, which is why most design presentations are walkthroughs — an inventory of what was made, in the order it was made.
**Demands:** Any skill that touches slides checks that the narrative exists first. Any skill that touches narrative checks that the why exists first.

### 4. Slides last, on purpose
**Belief:** Everything is built before the deck exists so the deck can never become a crutch.
**Why:** For designers this is the whole book. The deck is not the work, and it is not the portfolio. If you can't hold the room on words alone, the screens are doing your job for you — and the moment someone says "can you go back to the previous one," so are they.
**Demands:** Be able to deliver the whole thing with the screen off. If you can't, you're not ready to design slides.

### 5. Two realities
**Belief:** Every story has a world before and a world after. The story carries the room from one to the other.
**Why:** Duarte's "what is / what could be"; McKee's gap. Without a before, the after has no weight — the room sees a nice screen and shrugs. Without an after, there's no reason to fund it. This is also the visual brief: FROM on the left, TO on the right, one idea per slide.
**Demands:** Name the before and the after in one sentence each, from the user's point of view, before the design's. If either is missing, you don't have a story yet.

### 6. You're hardwired to be bad at this
**Belief:** Two evolutionary reasons, plus one that comes with the job.
**Why:** You know too much about your own work to see what a stranger needs (curse of knowledge). You speak to the neocortex when the croc brain is the gatekeeper — open with the flows and they question you; open with the need and they ask for the flows. And you were trained to let the work speak for itself. It doesn't. In a decision room, the work is mute until someone speaks for it.
**Demands:** Assume your first draft over-explains the how and under-explains the why. Get it in front of a PM or an engineer — never another designer — before you trust it.

### 7. A throughline outranks a collection of good ideas
**Belief:** One thread, or nothing lands.
**Why:** Cognitive load is real (Sweller). "And, and, and" is how competent designers lose rooms — every screen true, nothing memorable, and the VP leaves knowing you did a lot of work. The focusing question exists because five good points are worse than one.
**Demands:** State the story as a single sentence — and, but, therefore — before you write beats. Everything that doesn't serve it gets cut or moved to the appendix.

### 8. The audience, not you
**Belief:** The deck is for them. The goal is what they need to hear, not what you want to show. And this is also the fear cure.
**Why:** We can't focus inwards and outwards at the same time. Every bad deck is a fear made visible: too many screens is fear the work won't be valued unless all of it is seen; the deck-as-portfolio is fear that craft is being judged instead of the decision; the twelve-directions exploration slide is fear of committing. Your deck is gorgeous — that's why it failed.
**Demands:** Write every slide's message in the executive's voice, as the thought you want in their head: "Is this a real problem or a design problem?" "What does it cost me to say yes?" "Why now?" When you feel afraid, put your attention on them.

### 9. Fear is energy
**Belief:** Fear is learned, therefore controllable. It never goes away, and shouldn't.
**Why:** It's adrenaline. A racing mind is also a mind full of ideas; a racing heart is also focus; tense muscles are also readiness. The Tuesday-crit dread and the exec-review nausea are the same signal: you're about to do something that matters. The goal is not to speak without anxiety; it is to positively influence your audience.
**Demands:** Greet it — "this is me nervous because this decision matters" — then breathe, and put the energy into the room.

### 10. Practise is the key to all of it
**Belief:** Deliberate, out loud, in front of others, iterated on feedback. Not clicking through the file in your head.
**Why:** It hones the story, cures stage fright by systematic desensitisation, and makes you authentic because you know it cold. It's also the only way to find out whether the screens or the story did the talking: ask someone what three things they remember, and compare.
**Demands:** Pick a goal for the session, say it out loud, get feedback, repeat with someone outside the team. Don't let the exec review be the first time you say it to anyone.

### 11. Lean applied to storytelling
**Belief:** Pitch early, pitch often. Pitch → feedback → iterate → repeat.
**Why:** Build-measure-learn works on narratives exactly as it works on prototypes — designers already know this loop; they just don't apply it to the story. There is a minimum effective dose, and there is a point where more rehearsal makes you sound like a walkthrough again.
**Demands:** Get out of the design team. Then let good enough be good enough, step back, and leave it alone.

### 12. You are already an actor
**Belief:** You play lover, child, leader and colleague fluently every day. The presenter simply defaults to a role instead of choosing one.
**Why:** The designer's default is The Narrator — "and then the user taps here, and then this sheet comes up." The room needs The Owner — the person who made a call, knows what it cost, and is here for a decision. Same person, different role; mirror neurons do the rest.
**Demands:** Decide who you are in this room before you walk in. It is not a performance with a right way to do it; it is a conversation, from the person who owns the work.

## One adopted rule that belongs with the beliefs

**Feedback is a gift, not a request for change.** (Corey Ford.) Say thank you. Separate the information from the emotion — "it feels unfinished" usually means "I don't know what you're asking me for." Decide what serves the decision and integrate that; trust your gut on the rest. This matters twice for designers: it is how you take crit, and it is how you stop a crit from becoming a redesign session.

## The five questions

Asked in order, before any drafting. Every tactical skill starts here.

1. Who is this for, and what do they already believe about this work?
2. What is the one thing they must believe by the end — and what decision does that unlock?
3. What is the world before, and the world after, for the user?
4. What proof makes the change credible — research, prototype, test, number?
5. What room is this, and what shape does that room need it in?

## The diagnostics

Quick tests any skill can run against a draft.

- **Why-check** — can you say "I designed X so that Y" with no feature and no metric in the Y?
- **Two-realities test** — is there a clear before, a clear after, and a turn between them — for the user, not the interface?
- **Everyone test** — if the audience is "leadership," the audience isn't defined. Which leader, and what were they grilled on last quarter?
- **Throughline test** — one sentence, and-but-therefore; does every beat serve it?
- **So-what test** — after every point, ask so what; "we used a bottom sheet" → so what? → "a first-time user never leaves the flow."
- **Need-first test** — if it opens with screens, it opens on the wrong brain.
- **Three-things test** — does what listeners recall match what you intended, or did they only remember the screens?
- **Ninety-second test** — would this slide survive if the VP had ninety seconds?
- **Cue-card test** — if you can't deliver without the file, practise more.
- **Bluff test** — any answer you couldn't back with research or a number afterwards is a bluff.
- **Good-enough test** — roughly twenty hours or sixty reps, or a version that gets the decision even if you don't love it.
